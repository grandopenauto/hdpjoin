What Should You Automate First? A Readiness Checklist

The best first automation is usually not the most impressive demo. It is a repeatable task with known inputs, clear rules, recoverable failures and a result that matters to a customer or operator.

Published October 11, 2026 · Highest Degree Priorities

Begin with the bottleneck, not a model

Common candidates include copying approved information between systems, sorting routine inbound requests, preparing predictable reports, and flagging missing records. Less suitable first projects include unstructured decisions with no accountable owner, sensitive actions without approval gates, or processes that nobody agrees how to perform.

Ten readiness questions

For each question, answer Yes, Partly or No. This is a working discussion checklist, not a validated diagnostic score.

  1. Purpose: can someone define the task's expected output in one sentence?
  2. Repetition: does it happen often enough for improvement to matter?
  3. Inputs: are the inputs accessible, permitted and consistently structured?
  4. Rules: can the decision logic be described or bounded?
  5. Exceptions: do we know when to stop and ask a human?
  6. Ownership: is one person accountable for accepting the result?
  7. Permissions: are data, vendor access and consent requirements clear?
  8. Evidence: can we record success, failure and recovery without exposing sensitive information?
  9. Economics: can we estimate labor saved or value created after operating costs?
  10. Fallback: can the old workflow take over if automation stops?

Choose a bounded pilot

Pick a task with strong answers across most criteria. If ownership or authorized access is missing, address that before building. Treat unclear exceptions as a design problem—not a reason to make the automation guess.

For a first pilot, define what is in scope, what is prohibited, and what evidence will be reviewed. Example: automatically classify approved incoming service requests for human review, but do not send outbound communications or change customer account settings.

A suggested 30-day sequence

  • Week 1: baseline, map rules and define acceptance tests.
  • Week 2: sandbox or shadow-mode implementation with no unrestricted external actions.
  • Week 3: review exceptions, tune rules, validate logs and demonstrate fallback.
  • Week 4: review evidence and choose to stop, continue in scope or propose an approved expansion.
An automation isn't ready because it can run. It's ready when it can be trusted, supervised and recovered.
