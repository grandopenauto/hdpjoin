Build It, Buy It, or Have Someone Operate It?

The purchase price of software is only the beginning. There is also setup, training, vendor coordination, monitoring, unexpected exceptions and the question of who owns the outcome. Here's a practical way to compare three common routes.

Published October 11, 2026 · Highest Degree Priorities

Three different decisions

Build: create or customize software for the required business process. This can support differentiation and control, but carries development, security, documentation and maintenance obligations.

Buy: adopt an existing product. This often shortens initial implementation but involves licenses, configuration choices, vendor dependencies, data portability and feature constraints.

Operate: hire or use a managed service to take responsibility for a defined function and its ongoing evidence. Managed delivery may use built software, purchased software or both.

A worked decision: online booking for a service business

Suppose a business receives requests through phone calls and emails. Buying a scheduling product may solve the booking interface quickly. Building a custom portal may make sense if there are unusual routing requirements. A managed operation becomes valuable when someone needs to keep schedules accurate, chase missing information, recover failures and report conversions.

The decision depends on what is special: the booking interface itself, the workflow around it, or who will operate and improve the process after launch.

Calculate total cost of operation

Use a common time horizon—typically a quarter or year—for each option. The formula below is not a quote; it is a checklist of categories worth estimating.

Total cost = setup + subscriptions + usage + integration + human review + support + downtime exposure + transition cost.

A seemingly inexpensive agent can become expensive when it requires multiple speech, messaging, automation and model providers. Equally, a custom build can be economical when the alternative involves repetitive labor and several recurring vendors. Only real quotes and usage data tell the difference.

Ask for proof and control

  • Is the scope and acceptance test written down?
  • Who owns the domain, customer accounts, source and data?
  • Are third-party costs itemized rather than buried?
  • What can the system do without approval, and what requires a person?
  • Can the process be exported, paused or migrated?
  • What happens if the supplier or API becomes unavailable?

A fourth path: combine approaches

Many businesses benefit from buying standard components, building only the parts that differentiate them, and operating the resulting process with clear human supervision. HDP's Business Operating Systems concept is designed around this mix of modular components, third-party options where compatible, and bounded services.
