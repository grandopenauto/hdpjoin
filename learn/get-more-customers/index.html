How do I get more customers?

You may not need another complicated marketing campaign. Start by making it easier for the right people to find you, understand your offer, contact you and hear back.

Published October 22, 2026 · Highest Degree Priorities

Start with the 7-day plan ↓Explore the bigger opportunity ↗

Here is the short answer: find the people most likely to buy, show up where they look, give them a compelling reason to choose you, and follow through reliably. You can improve each step without replacing your entire business.

1. Know which customers you actually want

"More customers" is an incomplete target. A high-volume customer who needs a deep discount may be less valuable than a repeat customer who is easy to serve. Write down your best-fit buyer, the problem they already want solved, and the one result you can realistically deliver.

  • Audience: whom do you serve best—industry, geography, budget or urgency?
  • Offer: what do they get, how quickly, and what is included?
  • Evidence: what can you show today: examples, a demo, qualifications, published work, or a clear process?

2. Be present when someone is already looking

Check how your business appears in ordinary searches for your service and location. Make sure your website explains the actual services, service area, contact method and relevant details. If eligible, keep your Google Business Profile accurate. Avoid keyword-stuffed pages that say nothing new.

For example, a local service business may benefit more from a clear "emergency repair in [service area]" page than a generic claim that it handles everything. An online B2B provider may need a specific page for the problem its ideal customer is trying to solve.

3. Make the next step obvious

Once a visitor arrives, don't force them to hunt for a phone number, an estimate request or a demo. Use one primary action that matches the reason they came. Say what happens after they click or call, what information you need, and whether the consultation is free or paid.

4. Respond to the interest you've already earned

Missed inquiries, unanswered emails and forgotten follow-ups can make an acquisition problem look bigger than it is. Test your own customer experience: call after hours, submit the website form and see what happens. Record the time to first useful response.

5. Ask existing customers for the next introduction

After a successful, verified delivery, request a review or referral in a natural, appropriate way. Make referrals easy: a brief description of who you help and a direct link to the right contact page. Do not buy fake reviews or promise incentives in exchange for positive ratings.

6. Follow up with permission and a purpose

Some customers need time to make a decision. A short, useful follow-up can answer an unanswered question, offer the next step or clarify the proposal. Respect opt-outs and applicable consent rules; a larger automated message volume is not the same as better sales.

7. Measure the full journey, not only traffic

Track qualified inquiries → conversations → proposals → won work. Then look backward. If inquiries are scarce, improve discovery and relevance. If inquiries arrive but nobody answers, fix intake and response. If conversations happen but nobody purchases, inspect the offer, proof and proposal.

A realistic seven-day customer-growth plan

  1. Day 1: define your most profitable or best-fit customer and one specific service.
  2. Day 2: search for that service as a buyer would; document what is clear or missing.
  3. Day 3: improve one website/service page and its contact action.
  4. Day 4: test your contact form, phone, voicemail and follow-up process.
  5. Day 5: reconnect with legitimate past inquiries and request feedback from satisfied customers where appropriate.
  6. Day 6: assemble one real example, demonstration or clearly described process as proof.
  7. Day 7: review inquiry counts and response times, then choose one bottleneck to address next.

Should you buy software to solve this?

Not necessarily. Fix the simple, human and operational issues first. Software is useful when it removes a repeatable bottleneck and someone remains accountable for the result.
